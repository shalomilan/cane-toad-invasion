Beyond the records: estimating where invasive cane toads are, and where they are heading

Biodiversity records show where people looked, not where species live. I built a model that separates the two to estimate where invasive cane toads actually are in Australia, and tested it against ten years of data it had never seen.

Ilán Shalom · October 2026 · Code and notebooks

Exploratory analysis, not peer-reviewed. Every number on this page comes from reproducible notebooks with public data.

Tested on the past

Trained only with data up to 2015, the model predicted the next ten years better than a model of records alone.

Cane toads (Rhinella marina) were released in Queensland in 1935 and have spread across northern Australia, poisoning native predators along the way. Records from citizen science and surveys trace the invasion, but a record needs two things: toads must be there, and someone must find and report them. In remote areas, toads can be present for years before the first record.

This project models those two processes separately. Toads arrive in a cell depending on distance to occupied cells, climate and region; once there, they are detected depending on how many people record frogs in that place and year. The model was tested by training it with data up to 2015 and up to 2020 and asking it to predict what happened until 2025.

Three results stand out. Detection is very low where few people look (3–8% per year), so toads probably occupy about 40% more land than the records show. The invaded area will keep growing, but more slowly than in the past as the invasion reaches drier land. And a simpler model that treated records as presence looked good on headline numbers but gave worse probabilities: getting the totals right is not the same as getting the map right.

Methods

Data

  • Cane toad records: 73,293 records from the Atlas of Living Australia, cleaned to 37,719 (with year and coordinates, uncertainty under 10 km), aggregated to a 25 × 25 km grid of 12,312 cells.
  • Survey effort: 1.84 million records of other amphibians in the same cells and years: if people recorded frogs there, they were looking.
  • Climate: WorldClim 2.1 bioclimatic variables (Fick & Hijmans 2017); Köppen arid classes (Peel et al. 2007); major rivers from HydroRIVERS (Lehner & Grill 2013).
  • Native range: GBIF records of R. marina in South America (doi:10.15468/dl.zh2ft4), east of the Andes, the species in the strict sense after Acevedo et al. (2016).

The model: arrival and detection

This follows the logic of occupancy models with imperfect detection (MacKenzie et al. 2002), in their dynamic form with colonisation (MacKenzie et al. 2003). The arrival year is never observed: the first record happens at arrival plus a detection delay, so the likelihood sums over every possible arrival year.

  • Detection is a logistic regression fitted on the years after each cell's first record, when toads are known to be there.
  • Arrival is a discrete-time hazard model (a logistic regression on a cell × year table), fitted by maximum likelihood with the arrival year as a latent variable. Its predictors include climate suitability from a presence-background model and region-specific terms, because the invasion moved two to four times faster outside Queensland than inside it.
  • Uncertainty: a spatial block bootstrap (blocks of 250 × 250 km resampled, both models refitted 30 times); each of the 200 forecast simulations uses one refitted model.

How it was tested

True presence is never observed, so the model is judged on what it implies for records. These are the checks, from the most to the least important:

  • Backtests without leakage. Everything (climate suitability, detection, arrival, bootstrap) was refitted with data up to 2015, and again up to 2020, and the invasion was simulated year by year to 2025. Future survey effort is unknown to a forecaster, so it was frozen at its level in the three years before the cut-off.
  • Proper scoring rules (Gneiting & Raftery 2007). The log loss is the average of −log(probability given to what actually happened): a cell that got a record and had been given 90% costs 0.11; one given 1% costs 4.6. It rewards confident correct forecasts and punishes confident mistakes; lower is better. The Brier score is the mean squared difference between probability and outcome.
  • Comparison with a simpler model (first records only, no detection), on the same cells and years, with a paired spatial block bootstrap: 250-km blocks are resampled 2,000 times to get a 95% interval for the difference. If the interval excludes zero, the improvement is not noise from a few places.
  • Calibration curves: of the cells given a probability p, about p should get a record.
  • Error by forecast horizon: the model trained up to 2015 is scored on each year from 2016 (1 year ahead) to 2025 (10 years ahead).
  • Sensitivity: a version where desert cells cannot be colonised. It was indistinguishable from the main model, so only the main model is shown.
  • Spatial block cross-validation for the climate model (Roberts et al. 2017), and a check of the no-extinction assumption (detection in occupied cells by climate zone).

Key findings along the way

  1. Front speed. The western front advanced 43 km/yr in 1996–2025 (95% CI 40–47), estimated by Theil–Sen regression over 50-km strips (Sen 1968), consistent with the accelerating front described by Phillips et al. (2006).
  2. Climate suitability. Four presence-background models with target-group background (Phillips et al. 2009) tied under spatial block cross-validation (Roberts et al. 2017).
  3. Native range. The Australian niche barely overlaps the native one (Schoener's D = 0.09; Broennimann et al. 2012): toads in Australia live in much drier climates, as Tingley et al. (2014) found.
  4. A model of records. It placed the 2025 front within 12.5 km, but partly for the wrong reason, and it colonised the west far too slowly. Adding survey effort fixed the totals but not the probabilities. That failure motivated the arrival + detection model.

Results

  • Toads are in more places than the records show. About 560 cells of 25 × 25 km (≈350,000 km²) probably have toads but no record yet: about 1 in 3 occupied cells. Where nobody records frogs, toads are recorded only 3–8% of the years they are present.
  • Occupied area: about 1.15 million km² in 2025 (0.81 million with records), 1.26 million by 2030 and 1.36 million by 2035 (90% interval 1.33–1.44 million).
  • The model beats a model of records only on data it never saw: log loss 9–11% lower and AUC 0.87 vs 0.83 (trained up to 2015), and it reproduces the number of new cells (316 predicted vs 313 observed).
  • The probabilities are believable up to about 30%; above that, the forecast is overconfident, mostly because nobody knows in advance where people will look for frogs.

Where nobody records frogs, a toad population can go unrecorded for a decade or more. The model estimates that about 560 cells without records probably have toads (266 of them with a probability above 50%), including 86 "holes" surrounded by recorded cells.

Backtest: predicting 2016–2025 and 2021–2025 without seeing them

In both backtests the arrival + detection model has a lower log loss than the model of records only, and the paired spatial block bootstrap confirms it: the 95% interval of the difference is entirely below zero (−0.021 to −0.001 trained up to 2015; −0.017 to −0.004 trained up to 2020).

How the error grows with the forecast horizon

Errors of each kind. Turned into yes/no at 50%, the model would flag few cells (precision 32%, recall 4% trained up to 2015): almost every cell that got its first record had been given less than 50%. That is expected, not a failure: first records are rare and spread over many cells with modest probabilities, which is why the model is judged on its probabilities, not on a threshold. A "false positive" for records may also be a true presence that nobody has recorded yet.

Limitations

  • No local extinction. Once toads arrive, the model keeps them. In occupied semi-arid cells, toads are recorded 22% less often than expected, which could mean local extinctions in droughts or just lower densities. On the cool southern front the toads' range even shrank in some decades (Macgregor et al. 2021).
  • Long jumps are underpredicted: beyond the 2015 invasion front, 58 cells got records in 2016–2025 and the model expected 16.
  • Neighbourhood predictors use recorded cells, not true presence, which is unknown.
  • Future survey effort is assumed constant at its 2023–2025 level.
  • Static climate (1970–2000 normals): no climate change, no wet and dry years. The climate model is not part of the bootstrap.
  • Artificial water (dams, bores, troughs), which lets toads persist in arid land (Tingley et al. 2013), is not a predictor: the available national dataset is incomplete.
  • The forecast in the arid west is an extrapolation: toads have not yet met that climate, so no data can validate it.
  • Records for 2025 are probably incomplete because of upload lags; the 25-km grid hides local habitat.
